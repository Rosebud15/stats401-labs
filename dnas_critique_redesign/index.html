Visualization Critique and Redesign Report

Approximately 770 words

Original visualization and context

Duke Kunshan University's Division of Natural and Applied Sciences (DNAS) research page uses an interactive visualization to organize faculty by broad research area and more specific research topic. The interface can switch between a force-directed network and a collapsible hierarchical tree. Its intended message is that DNAS research is organized into recognizable areas while still containing relationships and overlap among topics and faculty. Likely audiences include current and prospective students looking for research mentors, faculty seeking collaborators, and visitors trying to understand the division's research landscape. Important tasks include locating a professor, finding researchers in a topic, comparing areas, understanding the hierarchy, and identifying interdisciplinary connections.

The reconstructed hierarchy contains 60 unique faculty and 29 area-topic groups. Additional public graph data provide richer faculty metadata, including research interests, research classifications, teaching-major support, and courses taught. I use those fields in the redesign to make search and faculty lookup more useful, rather than treating them as new quantitative measures of research quality or productivity.

Strengths

The original interface has two notable strengths. First, it offers multiple representations of the same data. The force-directed view emphasizes connectedness, while the tree gives hierarchical position a clear meaning: users can follow Area → broad research area → topic → faculty. Second, the tree uses progressive disclosure. Instead of showing every faculty member immediately, users expand only the branches relevant to them. The network also supports zooming, dragging, searching, and clickable nodes. These features show that the original is designed for exploration rather than being purely decorative.

Critique

The central weakness is that the two views divide useful tasks rather than supporting them together. The network provides a broad overview but is visually dense. Its force-generated positions are not a direct data encoding, so viewers may interpret proximity as meaningful even when it partly results from simulation forces. Large translucent circles used for major research pillars dominate the visual hierarchy even though their area does not clearly encode a quantitative value. Faculty labels also compete with nodes and links, making lookup difficult. Additionally, when looking up information, the visualization only changes the color of tiny points, which disagrees with the research on visual salience and makes it easy to miss the result. Moreover, faculty labels are color-encoded, but there is no legend or explanation for the color scheme.

The tree solves some of those problems but creates others. Parent-child relationships are clearer, yet each hierarchical level requires another horizontal column. A short path such as Area → Biological, behavioral sciences and health → Biology → Yiu Wing Kam can therefore stretch across much of the page while large regions remain empty. Progressive disclosure also makes comparison dependent on interaction and memory: comparing molecular biology with artificial intelligence requires opening separate branches and remembering what was previously shown. Useful summaries such as topic counts or faculty-membership counts are not displayed directly. Finally, repeated faculty in different branches can indicate interdisciplinarity, but the duplication itself is not explained.

Redesign rationale

My first redesign decision was to replace the single branching canvas with four faceted area panels. Research topics remain grouped within each area, so the hierarchy is preserved through enclosure and alignment rather than large horizontal offsets. This keeps substantially more of the structure visible at once and makes cross-area comparison easier.

Second, I added direct counts and compact bars. The redesign reports faculty memberships and topic counts instead of requiring viewers to count nodes. A separate overview chart makes differences among the four areas easier to compare without implying that visual area represents importance.

Third, I made interdisciplinary membership explicit. Faculty who appear in more than one top-level area in the public tree receive an ↗ marker; selecting a faculty member highlights every occurrence and lists each area-topic membership. The richer faculty data are also revealed on selection: the detail panel shows research interests, primary and secondary research classifications, teaching-major support, listed courses, email, and faculty profile link. Search now matches not only names and topics but also research interests, course codes, and major-support fields. This supports a realistic student task such as finding faculty connected to “IoT” or a particular course without adding permanent clutter to the overview.

Original vs. redesign and limitations

Compared with the original, the redesign makes overview, lookup, and cross-category comparison easier because the major areas remain visible together and text labels are primary marks rather than annotations on a network. It sacrifices some of the organic network appearance and intentionally repeats faculty names to preserve local context. Coordinated highlighting makes those repeated identities explicit. The source files also contain inconsistent capitalization and some research/teaching taxonomy labels that do not align perfectly; I preserve those values rather than silently correcting them. The redesign therefore communicates membership and recorded metadata, not research intensity, publication volume, collaboration strength, or faculty performance.
