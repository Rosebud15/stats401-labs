Lab 9: Geospatial Visualization

Global GDP, two ways

How does the picture change when GDP controls a country's color instead of its visual area? Compare an undistorted geographic map with a proportional-area Dorling cartogram using the same 2025 nominal GDP data.

1. Dataset & encodings

The provided dataset contains the 50 largest economies by 2025 nominal GDP, reported in billions of current U.S. dollars and sourced to the IMF's October 2025 World Economic Outlook. WEO values can include estimates and may be revised. ISO-3 identifiers join GDP records to Natural Earth country boundaries.

Countries outside the supplied top 50 are gray (no data) in the choropleth, not zero GDP. The source's low-resolution boundaries omit separate polygons for Singapore and Hong Kong, so these are explicitly displayed as location markers, not invented country shapes.

4 · Design & comparison

What changes when we encode GDP with area?

The United States and China stand out in both views. On the choropleth, they appear among the darkest countries, but their prominence is still influenced by how much land they occupy. Russia, Canada, and Australia cover large geographic regions even though their GDP values are far smaller. Smaller high-GDP economies such as Germany, Japan, and the United Kingdom can be harder to spot because their geographic shapes occupy relatively little space.

The Dorling cartogram replaces real country shapes with circles. The United States and China become the largest circles, while countries with large land areas but smaller GDP shrink relative to them. Germany, Japan, and the United Kingdom become more noticeable relative to their physical size. Circle area is proportional to GDP—not radius—so an economy with twice the GDP gets twice the area. Repelling overlapping circles shifts some countries from their exact locations, particularly in crowded Europe and East Asia.

The choropleth is useful for locating countries, observing broad regional patterns, and seeing which economies have higher GDP while preserving coastlines and borders. The cartogram instead supports comparing overall economic magnitude without conflating it with land area, at the cost of recognizable shapes, exact positions, and some neighborhood relationships. Linked highlighting reconnects the two representations when individual circles are difficult to identify.
